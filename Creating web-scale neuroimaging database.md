Creating a web-scale neuroimaging database (“growing YouTube-scale brain data”)  
*Alternate title: Scaling up brain data to make neuroimaging useful to core AI* 

**Current EPI implementation (0.3.0).** The first operational route is deliberately narrower than the full vision: a researcher installs one terminal executable and runs `neuro-sync /path/to/completed-dicoms`. The workstation selects only fixture-verified functional EPI, recursively de-identifies DICOM metadata, retains scanner-native Pixel Data and reviewed acquisition/geometry fields, and uploads deterministic per-series archives with automatic continuation. Cloudflare R2 holds this privacy-cleared DICOM as the canonical source; Sophont asynchronously performs pinned conversion and scientific validation to create NIfTI and minimized metadata derivatives. Structural MRI, diffusion, and uncertain scanner families remain local. A future watcher can automate repeated folder discovery, and structural imaging will use a separate route with validated defacing and brain-preservation QC rather than weakening the EPI path.

Data is considered the “fossil fuel of AI” \[1\]. Yet, relative to modalities like image, text, and audio, there is a marked scarcity of neural data available to train AI models. Further, researchers currently training on neural data face innumerable barriers: lengthy applications, costly fees, strict security restrictions, standardization issues, storage costs, etc. Our plan is to create the largest ever neuroimaging database that addresses these problems and adheres to privacy & security protocols.	 			Consider how text is an impoverished representation of internal thought, compressed and degraded in order to be used for communication. AI models would improve if we could train using data modalities with richer representations. Brain data fits the bill, containing rich signals that later on are reduced and funneled into our sensory outputs like text and speech. Further, aligning AI with human brain data should naturally guide models towards more optimal objective functions \[2\].  
We first focus on fMRI brain scans (“Why brain MRI?” section on page 3). We will develop a script to detect relevant scans created at MRI facilities and securely copy de-identified data to a cloud storage provider. The key is to ensure a robust script that can run automatically in the background without researcher intervention. We would arrange agreements with existing MRI facilities that already collect brain scans[^1]. By framing this project as a means to massively scale-up open neuroimaging data for the advancement of neuroAI, and by granting access to this dataset to any labs contributing data to it, we believe that academics would voluntarily be open to such agreements (we can also pay facilities in exchange for setup inconveniences).   
MRI facilities currently collect terabytes of data every day across the US and nearly all of it is never released. As a former postdoc at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute, I know that Princeton collects approximately 25 hours of fMRI data weekly. This means that Princeton alone creates \~1,300 hours fMRI data per year. We could collect roughly 30K hours of fMRI data every year if we simply arranged data sharing agreements with 23 similar academic MRI facilities (we have permission to start this process at Princeton). Coincidentally, 30K hours is the estimated total amount of fMRI data aggregated across *all datasets ever released* \[2\]. Therefore, this plan aims to cheaply share more fMRI data annually than the total aggregated fMRI data ever released since the technology’s introduction in the 1990s. Consider the staggering impact of the ImageNet dataset–this project aims to drive a comparable breakthrough by harnessing large-scale unlabeled neural data to enhance core AI models.   
We’ve already begun training a self-supervised foundation brain model on existing publicly accessible fMRI data ([video of model training](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1aPm-xPxtOeY_FAR80dnKbec6dty_YRdC/view?usp=drive_link)). We adopt the self-supervised masked autoencoder framework that performs well for video foundation models \[3\], as we hypothesize that applying such a framework to spatiotemporal human brain imaging data will yield similarly good results. Such a model allows new brain scans to be transformed into "aligned" latent representations and used as a drop-in replacement for standard voxel-space representations. Our plan is to develop both the data sharing script and foundation modeling in tandem, as improvements in either can inform best practices for the other. Such a foundation model will be necessary to best process new brain scans into latents that are then used by researchers for downstream fine-tuning of core AI models.   
Our goal is to (1) efficiently and cheaply scale up neural data, (2) use this data to fuel training of large-scale brain foundation models, (3) apply foundation models to well-curated paired neuro datasets to fine-tune core AI models; proof-of-concept for this approach comes from existing fMRI work that show improvements to core AI models \[4,5,6,7\]. By scaling up neural data and creating brain foundation models we will prove the practical relevance of brain data to AI and use this to break through scaling law walls \[1\].   
***Costs***  
Creation of large-scale neuroimaging dataset

- Develop data sharing script  
- Networking to arrange data sharing agreements   
- Paying partnered MRI facilities for set-up installation  
- Cloud storage costs for shared data (500TB \= $3,000 monthly)  
- Total: \~$75K yearly (not including employee costs)  
- **Compare this to how much it would cost to create our own dataset: \~$1,000 per hour of MRI scanning means that 30K hours of data would cost $30M.**

Training foundation model \+ downstream applications to core AI

- Compute \+ storage fees to train and evaluate models  
- Assuming $2.50 H100 hourly rate  
  - 8 H100s always active (8,760 x 2.50 x 8\) \= $175,200  
  - More GPUs on-demand for large-scale training runs  
  - 20% overhead  
- Total: $700K yearly

***Q\&A***  
**Why is web-scale neural data important?** Already there exist work showing core AI models can be improved with the help of existing scarce brain data: speech models like Whisper improve from fine-tuning on fMRI from people listening to podcasts \[4\]; object segmentation models improve by fine-tuning on fMRI from people looking at images \[5\]; scaling laws hold when applied to neuroAI models  \[6,7\]; my lab has preliminary results showing how a foundation model we train on unlabeled fMRI improves performance on MindEye \[8,9\] fMRI-to-image reconstruction. Web-scale neural data will enable even more obvious benefits. Neural data provides richer guidance than behavior and we believe that brain data for AI alignment will become increasingly useful to leading AI organizations as they race towards AGI.     
**Why unlabeled data?** Academics usually only release their data after successful publication and only if they generously support open science. We can scale up data rapidly and cheaply by simply automating data sharing immediately after scan completions. This strategy means that shared data will be unlabeled, but this is actually beneficial because it ensures that researchers cannot be “scooped” by sharing their data prior to publication. Unlabeled data is routinely responsible for AI breakthroughs (e.g., self-supervised training of models like GPT on web-scale text data), and we believe similar breakthroughs will be possible with the release of massive neuroimaging data.

### **Why brain MRI?** We think this strategy would also work for other modalities like EEG, MEG, iEEG, etc. We target fMRI (and other brain MRI modalities like T1w and DWI) first because it is the only modality shown to improve core AI models so far, is the highest spatial resolution non-invasive human neuroimaging modality, and can be scaled up rapidly via collaboration with academic institutions. In the future we can explore linking different brain modalities together via multimodal foundation models.

**Addressing data size.** ImageNet’s success can be attributed not only to its large scale but also to how it is easy to access and download locally. Existing fMRI datasets are painful to work with: setting aside costly and time-consuming data sharing agreements, fMRI data consumes a ton of storage space. fMRI scans are represented as NIfTI files, a medical imaging format consisting of a 3d cube containing brain activity over time. Just 1 hour of fMRI can consume \~30GB in NIfTI format, making it difficult for academics to work with massive datasets. We plan to reduce dataset size by 30x by taking advantage of the fact that neuroAI models typically only care about gray matter in the cerebral cortex. By reformatting NIfTI files into paired flattened arrays, one array listing gray matter voxel signal and one array listing 3d spatial coordinates, we can share low-size large-scale brain datasets easily accessible by researchers. We will also retain privacy-cleared scanner-native DICOM pixels and reviewed acquisition metadata for researchers who need to reinterpret or reprocess the canonical source data.
**Aren’t existing efforts like UKBB, HCP, and OpenNeuro enough?** Efforts to create large-scale neuroimaging datasets like OpenNeuro, HCP, UKBB, etc. are fantastic and have obviously benefitted the field, but the scale of accessible neuroimaging data is still relatively small in comparison to web-scale datasets (e.g., compare AI leaders training video models on all of YouTube). The approach of funding new large-scale datasets or waiting for generous neuroimagers to share their data on OpenNeuro are not going to scale to the same extent. To scale efficiently, we aim to arrange a system of data sharing agreements across institutions such that new neuroimaging data are automatically deidentified and copied to a communal database immediately after scan completion.  
**Is this commercializable?** Our strategy is to use large-scale neuroimaging data to train our own foundation model (and downstream models) to ultimately demonstrate practically useful core AI applications. Once we demonstrate the meaningful benefits of brain data to improve core AI models, we expect AI companies to be interested in paid collaboration both in terms of collecting new data for fine-tuning and for utilizing our foundation model.
